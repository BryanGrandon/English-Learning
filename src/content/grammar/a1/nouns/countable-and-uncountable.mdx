Nouns can be countable or uncountable. This affects how we use them with numbers, articles, plural forms, and quantifiers.

---

## Countable Nouns

Countable nouns are nouns for things we can _count using numbers_. They have both _singular and plural forms_.
We use countable nouns for **people, animals, objects, and other things** that we can count.

### Singular Form

We use a singular countable noun to talk about one person, animal, object, or thing.

<LessonBlock type='rule' title='A, An or One'>
A singular countable noun can be used with a, an, or the number one.

- a book
- an umbrella
- one book

</LessonBlock>

---

### Plural Form

We use plural countable nouns to talk about two or more people, animals, objects, or things.
Plural countable nouns can be used with numbers, many, and other plural determiners.

<LessonBlock type='example' title='Examples'>

- She has **three dogs**.
- I own **a house**.
- **How many friends** do you have?

</LessonBlock>

---

## Uncountable Nouns

Uncountable nouns are nouns for things that we _cannot count using numbers_.

They are often used for **substances**, **materials**, **ideas**, and **qualities** that we usually cannot count as individual items.

<ContentGrid>
<LessonBlock type='rule' title='No Plural Form'>

Uncountable nouns usually _do not have a plural form_ and are used with a **singular verb**.

- water
- milk
- rice
- money
- information
- advice

</LessonBlock>

<LessonBlock type='structure' title='Expressing Quantity'>

To express a quantity, use words or expressions such as:

- **some** water
- **a lot of** money
- **much** information
- **a bit of** advice
- **a cup of** tea
- **1 kg of** rice
- **a pinch of** salt

</LessonBlock>
</ContentGrid>

<LessonBlock type='common_mistake' title={`Don't use a/an`} fullWidth>
  We don't normally use **_a/an_** directly with an **uncountable noun**.
</LessonBlock>

<LessonBlock type='example' title='Examples'>

- There has been **a lot of research** into the causes of this disease.
- He gave me **a great deal of advice** before my interview.
- Can you give me **some information** about uncountable nouns?
- He did not have **much sugar** left.
- Measure **1 cup of water**, **300 g of flour**, and **1 teaspoon of salt**.

</LessonBlock>

---

## Countable vs. Uncountable

Some nouns describe individual objects, while others describe substances, concepts, or things viewed as a whole.

<ContentGrid> 
<LessonBlock type='comparison' title='The Difference' fullWidth>

| Countable | Uncountable |
| --------- | ----------- |
| apple     | water       |
| book      | rice        |
| chair     | milk        |
| idea      | information |
| student   | money       |
| bottle    | advice      |

</LessonBlock>
<LessonBlock type='example'>

- I have two apples.
- I have some rice.
- She bought three books.
- She gave me some advice.

</LessonBlock>
</ContentGrid>

## Quantifiers

Different quantifiers are used with countable and uncountable nouns.

<LessonBlock type="comparison">

| Countable | Uncountable     | Both     |
| --------- | --------------- | -------- |
| many      | much            | some     |
| few       | little          | any      |
| a few     | a little        | a lot of |
| several   | a great deal of | lots of  |

</LessonBlock>

### Countable nouns

Use many, few, a few, and several with plural countable nouns.

- There are many students here.
- I have a few books.
- She has several friends.

### Uncountable nouns

Use much, little, and a little with uncountable nouns.

- We don't have much time.
- I have a little money.
- There is little water left.

### Both

Use some, any, a lot of, and lots of with both types.

- I have some books.
- I have some money.
- There are a lot of people here.
- There is a lot of water here.
