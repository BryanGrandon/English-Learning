The verbs have and have got can both be used to talk about possession, relationships, and characteristics.

In the present, they usually have the same meaning.

<LessonBlock type='example'>

- I have a car.
- I have got a car.

</LessonBlock>

Both mean the same thing.

---

## Affirmative

<LessonBlock type='structure' title='Present Forms' >

| Subject        | Have      | Have got |
| -------------- | --------- | -------- |
| I / You / We / | They have | have got |
| He / She / It  | has       | has got  |

- I have a dog. → I have got a dog.
- She has a brother. → She has got a brother.

</LessonBlock>

## Have vs Have got

<LessonBlock type='comparison' title='Key Difference' fullWidth>

- Both forms are commonly used for possession.
- Have is used in both British and American English.
- Have got is especially common in British English and informal speech.

</LessonBlock>

<LessonBlock type='common_mistake' title='Avoid This Mistake' fullWidth>

After doesn't or does, use have, not has.

- ❌ She doesn't has a car.
- ✅ She doesn't have a car.

</LessonBlock>
